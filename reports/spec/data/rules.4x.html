A clause says which relation and what to do with it; a binding says which column takes which
input. They are two relations because a clause cannot carry residency's columns - those
change with the relation it points at, and a relation's columns do not change.

build-extractor says nothing about capacity. It removes a labor, removes a metal and adds
an extractor; that there must be a deposit with room for it is a fact about the world, stated
once in schema.4x and enforced wherever a world is checked. A rule that cannot break it is
better than a rule that remembers not to - and the next rule that makes an extractor is bound
by it without saying anything either.

work requires the deposit it reads from. The density is a column of that row, so the
require clause and the reading clause are the same one seen twice - and a territory holding two
grades of one resource cannot arise, because the structure refuses it.

A command names what is moved, not which row. Scouts are fungible, so what is the category
and from is what picks the row out of the three a category may have. There is no it: a
residency has no id to name, because it is keyed by its description.

require and remove are patterns; add is a whole row. The adjacency clause names no id
because it asks whether any road runs that way. The add clause names every column, and the
quantity is the one no caller supplies - an input is typed as a relation and carries one of its
keys, so a plain number cannot be one. {literal ...} is where it comes from, which is what
releases/first-release.md does with a Qty written in the recipe.

move binds places and adjacency joins territories, so the rule makes the join. Two
require place clauses match the places named, and the adjacency clause takes its from and its
to by reading each one's of - which is {reading ...}, the same machinery work uses for a
deposit's density and upkeep for a parent's bearing. Two sources feeding one pattern is what
row_of already does, because it walks every reading on a clause rather than one.

The places are required before the adjacency, and the order is why. A reading takes from the
row an earlier clause matched, and matched is filled in clause order during the require pass -
so a place named after the adjacency would be read before it had matched anything.

This is the join Sean chose. 2026-09-20: *I don't want to be "stating adjacency between
places".* The alternative was an adjacency row per pair of places, which is three times the rows
and a kind column to keep the three kinds of move apart. Rows in the world against readings in
the rule, and the readings keep the graph one thing.

The two places must share a layer, and that is what stops anything crossing. Sean, 2026-09-20:
for now I am thinking nothing will move between layers. The to place takes its layer from
the from place's, so a surface and an orbit never match and a move between them is refused -
by construction rather than by a rule saying no.

It is the same move as the territory join one line up. What would be an equality test is a
pattern: the value is read from the row the first clause matched and written into the second
clause's pattern, and a place that does not carry it is simply not found.

Crossing happens, but never as a move. Deploying an ark takes it out of an orbit and puts what
it becomes on the surface; building one takes resources off the surface and puts an ark in orbit.
Both are transformations rather than journeys, and neither is this rule.

what is typed as a relation and not as a family, which is what lets one rule reach a kind
and a family alike: {refresh what:scout trait:moving} denotes one relation, and
{refresh what:unit trait:moving} denotes every member of that family. The type cannot narrow
this to the kinds that carry the trait, because an input's type is fixed and which kinds carry
a trait depends on the other argument - so the pairing is refused when fired rather than when
declared, and offered shows only the pairs that would work.

{assigns ... input:trait ...} names an input where it named a column. The trait's name is
the column's name, so nothing else has to say which column: moving restores scout.moving
and extractor.moving does not exist, which is the refusal rather than a lookup that fails
quietly.

refresh has no where, because time does not visit one place. It had one while it was a
player's command; as a step of ending a turn it restores every count there is, and the pattern
its put matches names no place at all. Checked against the tests rather than assumed - the
four that refresh have one territory or one scout between them, so none of them could tell a
per-place refresh from this one, and none ever needed to.

Ending a turn is the composite, and refresh is its whole body so far. spec/turn.md has five
steps - upkeep, then growth or starvation, then what expires, then what nature reclaims, and
time restores every count to the number that thing's kind declares last. Only the last is
built, and the other four are rules this order has room for rather than something this rule
will grow clauses to do.

It takes no arguments, so its parts supply constants and nothing is passed down from a parent
to a child. That is the smallest thing that works; a part that needed its parent's argument would
be a second kind of {argument ...}, and nothing needs one yet.
